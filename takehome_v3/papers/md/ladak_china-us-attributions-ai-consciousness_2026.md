**Abstract**

Despite AI’s global reach, little is known about how laypeople outside Western contexts think about
whether AI systems can be conscious—capable of subjective experience—or whether they could have
moral status—warranting moral consideration. In a preregistered main and replication study, we
compared such attitudes among laypeople in China and the US (main sample: _N_ = 820; replication
sample: _N_ = 910). Across a range of measures, Chinese participants were more open to AI
consciousness and moral status than US participants. For example, a larger proportion of Chinese than
US participants judged it in-principle possible that AI could be conscious (86.3% vs. 39.6%) and have
moral status (89.1% vs. 54.0%). While both countries, on average, leaned against current AI systems
being conscious, Chinese participants’ average ratings were higher than US participants’. Chinese
participants also, on average, rated a hypothetical future AI system—an exact digital copy of a human
brain with a physical body—as having comparable consciousness and moral status to a chimpanzee,
while US participants, on average, rated the same system close to an ant. These differences may reflect
more positive general attitudes towards AI and greater animism (measured by the belief that non-living
things can have a spirit or soul) in China than in the US. Despite these diverging attitudes, participants in
neither country supported protecting advanced AI systems at the expense of humans or their own
country. Yet participants in both countries supported international cooperation regarding conscious AI,
pointing to an opportunity for collaboration in global AI governance.

**Introduction**

[The rapidly improving capabilities (1) and increasing human-likeness (2) of AI systems have sparked](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?bmE4hg)
debates about whether they are, or could become, conscious—capable of subjective experience, such as
pleasure or pain—and whether they have, or could come to have, moral status—warranting moral
[consideration (3–6). Alongside these scientific and philosophical debates, researchers have also begun to](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?65tc7h)
[investigate laypeople’s perceptions of AI consciousness (7,8). Such perceptions could have wide-ranging](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?iThcm6)
consequences regardless of how the expert debate is resolved, from affecting users’ relationships with
[other people (9) to influencing whether they support or oppose AI rights (10). Current evidence indicates](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?W9jQFm)
[that most laypeople in the US are skeptical of AI consciousness, both in current AI (11,12) and in more](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Qmqjhh)
[advanced, hypothetical systems (13,14). However, despite AI’s global reach (15), little is known about](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dWtFP6)
such perceptions in non-Western cultures.

Here, we compare perceptions of AI consciousness and moral status among laypeople in the US and
China. These two countries represent distinct, globally influential cultures that differ along multiple
[psychological and social dimensions (16,17). Additionally, they are both global leaders in AI development](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5wO3BC)
[(18) where hundreds of millions of people interact with AI (19,20). Comparing perceptions in China and](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UpPrNd)
the US therefore provides an opportunity to test whether the skepticism about AI consciousness among

1

laypeople in the US extends to another highly consequential cultural context, and to explore a variety of
possible explanations for any differences. This comparison could also provide practical insights into how
potentially conscious AI systems might be integrated into these societies, and how these systems might
need to be governed, both domestically and globally.

There are reasons to expect China–US differences in perceptions of AI consciousness and moral status.
First, there are differences in broader attitudes towards AI. For example, compared to the US public,
[people in China report greater excitement and less nervousness about AI (18), more optimism about AI’s](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?P0avAV)
[effects on social issues such as health and the economy (21), and higher overall trust in AI (22). These](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CMbrAO)
differences could reflect more positive media narratives around AI and greater exposure to AI in some
[East Asian versus Western countries (23,24). Second, there is existing evidence that people in China](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kTmmZU)
[attribute more mind to AI than people in the US—Yan et al. (25) found that Chinese participants attributed](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?i0HDlJ)
[more agency (the capacity to plan and act; 26) and experience (the capacity to sense and feel; 26) to a](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ggYI3N)
virtual human than US participants—and also have a greater tendency to anthropomorphize technology in
[general (27). Folk et al. (27) also compared Western and East Asian students at a Canadian university on](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WAGvT9)
a single-item measure of robot consciousness, and found greater attributions among the East Asian
students. This greater propensity to attribute mind to AI and technology in East Asian countries could
reflect a greater prevalence of folk animism: the view that all material things, living and non-living, contain
[spirit or soul (23,24,28,29). This perspective, often associated with Eastern traditions such as Buddhism](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0EXH1M)
[and Daoism (30), contrasts with Western traditions, which often emphasize a stronger distinction between](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?JGKIGQ)
[humans and other entities (31).](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fOSy8P)

These earlier findings point towards differences in AI consciousness attributions between China and the
US. However, comparisons of AI consciousness attributions between the two countries are limited and
unsystematic, and, as far as we are aware, no work has compared important potential downstream
effects, such as moral status attribution and governance attitudes. In the present work, we conducted a
preregistered online study (and a replication study; see Supplementary Materials) with samples recruited
from China ( _N_ = 431) and the US ( _N_ = 389) to address three research questions. First, are there China–
US differences in attributions of AI consciousness? We measured perceptions of current AI, more
advanced AI that could be created in the future, and the possibility of AI consciousness in principle.
Second, are there China–US differences in attributions of AI moral status? As with consciousness, we
considered current AI, future AI, and the in-principle possibility of AI moral status. We also compared
attributions to AI that is described as definitely conscious, given the importance of this criterion for moral
[status (32,33).](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1YhYlx) [1] Third, are there China–US differences in attitudes towards the governance of conscious
AI? This included whether such systems should be created at all, and how they should be governed if
they are built. In addition, we collected data on several exploratory measures that could explain
differences, including general AI attitudes and animism.

_**Attributions of consciousness**_

On average, Chinese participants were more likely than US participants to believe that current AI systems
(e.g., ChatGPT, DeepSeek) are conscious ( _d_ = 0.71, _p_ < .001). Still, both groups rated current AI lower in
consciousness than an ant ( _M_ China = 29.49 vs. 43.69; _M_ US = 13.58 vs. 44.03), though the difference
between current AI and ant ratings was smaller for Chinese participants than US participants ( _d_ = 0.40, _p_
< .001). When framed as a binary choice, a larger proportion of Chinese participants rated current AI
higher in consciousness than an ant (28.5% vs. 20.6%; Cramér’s _V_ = .09, _p_ = .010).

Chinese participants, on average, were more likely than US participants to think that AI consciousness is
possible in principle ( _d_ = 1.26, _p_ < .001), and a larger proportion of Chinese than US participants rated AI
consciousness as possible in a binary choice (86.3% vs. 39.6%; Cramér’s _V_ = 0.49, _p_ < .001).

Chinese participants, on average, were more likely than US participants to think that a hypothetical future
AI—an exact digital copy of a human brain with a physical body—would be conscious ( _d_ = 0.97, _p_ <
.001). Both groups rated this embodied, digital human above an ant ( _M_ China = 71.01 vs. 43.69; _M_ US =
46.07 vs. 44.03), but the difference was larger for Chinese participants ( _d_ = 0.57, _p_ < .001), and both
groups rated this system below a chimpanzee ( _M_ China = 71.01 vs. 74.64; _M_ US = 46.07 vs. 83.71), but the
difference was smaller for Chinese participants ( _d_ = 0.91, _p_ < .001). On binary measures, a larger
proportion of Chinese than US participants rated the embodied, digital human higher in consciousness
than an ant (75.6% vs. 44.0%) and a chimpanzee (48.7% vs. 12.9%; Cramér’s _V_ s = .32–.38, _p_ s < .001).

_**Attributions of moral status and rights**_

On average, Chinese participants were more likely than US participants to think current AI systems
warrant moral consideration ( _d_ = 0.74, _p_ < .001). Both groups rated current AI as less worthy of moral
consideration than an ant ( _M_ China = 41.64 vs. 47.02; _M_ US = 23.21 vs. 49.60), but the difference between
current AI and ant ratings was smaller for Chinese participants than US participants ( _d_ = 0.53, _p_ < .001).

Chinese participants, on average, were more likely than US participants to think that AI could warrant
moral consideration in principle ( _d_ = 0.98, _p_ < .001), and a larger proportion of Chinese participants rated
it as possible for AI to warrant moral consideration in a binary choice (89.1% vs. 54.0%; Cramér’s _V_ =
0.39, _p_ < .001).

Chinese participants rated an embodied, digital human more worthy of moral consideration than an ant
whereas US participants rated the same entity below an ant ( _M_ China = 70.71 vs. 47.02; _M_ US = 47.96 vs.
49.60), and the gap between the embodied, digital human and ant ratings differed significantly between
Chinese and US participants ( _d_ = 0.59, _p_ < .001). Both groups rated an embodied, digital human below a
chimpanzee ( _M_ China = 70.71 vs. 75.88; _M_ US = 47.96 vs. 81.99), but the difference was smaller for Chinese
participants ( _d_ = 0.78, _p_ < .001). While both groups, on average, favored preventing harm to one
biological human over 1000 digital humans in a hypothetical moral trade-off, Chinese participants were
less likely to favor the biological human ( _d_ = 0.41, _p_ < .001).

We also asked participants to imagine a future where conscious AI can definitely be created. Chinese
participants, on average, were more likely than US participants to rate definitely conscious AI as worthy of
moral consideration ( _d_ = 0.55, _p_ < .001). A larger proportion of Chinese participants also considered
conscious AI worthy of moral consideration (92.8% vs. 66.1%; Cramér’s _V_ = 0.33, _p_ < .001).

We asked participants whether conscious AI systems should be granted seven rights: protection from
harm, life, mental privacy, mental self-control, liberty, owning property and assets, and voting. Both
groups most strongly favored conscious AI’s right to protection from harm ( _M_ China = 5.72, _SD_ China = 1.19;
_M_ US = 5.01, _SD_ US = 1.93) and most strongly disfavored conscious AI’s right to vote ( _M_ China = 3.46, _SD_ China
= 1.63; _M_ US = 2.58, _SD_ US = 1.87). Chinese participants were more in favor than US participants of
granting conscious AI systems all of the seven rights ( _d_ s = 0.45–0.70, _p_ s < .001).

_**Governance and policy attitudes**_

Chinese participants were more supportive of creating conscious AI than US participants ( _d_ = 1.25, _p_ <
.001) and were more likely to think society would treat conscious AI well ( _d_ = 0.91, _p_ < .001). Both groups
supported international cooperation to govern conscious AI, though Chinese participants were more
supportive ( _d_ = 0.35, _p_ < .001), and both groups leaned against protecting conscious AI at a geopolitical
cost, with no significant differences between them ( _d_ = 0.09, _p_ = .187).

_**Exploratory analysis of mechanisms**_

To explore factors that might explain China–US differences, we compared individual difference measures,
tested their correlations with consciousness and moral status attributions, and conducted mediation
analysis. Compared to US participants, Chinese participants reported higher AI animism (measured by
whether AI can have a spirit or soul; _d_ = 1.55) and general animism (measured by whether any non-living
things can have a spirit or soul; _d_ = 0.29), greater openness to friendship with AI ( _d_ = 1.45), greater
likelihood of having experienced an emotional connection with AI ( _d_ = 1.11), greater experience with AI ( _d_
= 0.55), greater openness to social influence about AI consciousness ( _d_ = 0.80), more positive media
perceptions of AI ( _d_ = 0.76), more excitement about AI benefits ( _d_ = 0.77), and less concern about AI
risks ( _d_ = 1.07, all _p_ s < .001). There was no significant difference in our measure of mind–body dualism ( _d_
= 0.01, _p_ = .911).

To make these possible mechanisms more concrete, we report here the results of two serial mediation
models testing a theoretically plausible explanatory account. Both models included excitement about AI
benefits and general animism as parallel first-stage mediators. The first model included current AI
consciousness attribution as a second-stage mediator and current AI moral status attribution as the
dependent variable; the second model included belief in the possibility of AI consciousness and moral
status in the corresponding roles. Consistent with this account, the indirect effect of country (0 = _US_, 1 =
_China_ ) on moral status attribution via excitement about AI benefits and, in turn, consciousness attribution
was significant for both current AI moral status attribution ( _b_ = 0.09, 95% CI [0.06, 0.13]) and belief in the
possibility of AI moral status ( _b_ = 0.10, 95% CI [0.07, 0.15]), as was the equivalent indirect effect via
animism (current AI moral status: _b_ = 0.04, 95% CI [0.02, 0.07]; belief in the possibility of AI moral status:
_b_ = 0.04, 95% CI [0.02, 0.06]). Overall, the three mediators accounted for 58.7% of the total effect of
country on current AI moral status attribution (40.1% via paths involving AI benefits and animism
specifically, 18.6% via consciousness attribution's effect not explained by the first-stage mediators) and
82.3% of the total effect on belief in the possibility of AI moral status (32.8% and 49.5%, respectively).
The direct effect of country remained significant in both models (current AI moral status: _b_ = 0.38, 95% CI

[0.21, 0.55]; belief in the possibility of AI moral status: _b_ = 0.19, 95% CI [0.05, 0.33]). Note that due to the
cross-sectional nature of our data, these results are consistent with, but cannot establish, the theorized
causal pathways. See Table S13 for further details of the mediation analyses.

**Discussion**

Across a wide range of measures, Chinese participants were more open to AI consciousness and moral
status than US participants, often with large effect sizes. We found evidence of higher attributions among
Chinese participants for current AI and hypothetical future AI, as well as for the in-principle possibility of
AI consciousness and moral status. However, participants in neither country were in favor of protecting
advanced AI systems at the expense of human welfare or at a geopolitical cost. Notably, participants in
both countries supported international cooperation to govern conscious AI. These findings were robust to
sensitivity analyses and the main findings were replicated in a supplementary study.

One might question, however, whether these findings reflect genuine China–US differences in AI attitudes
or if instead they reflect different understandings of key concepts (e.g., consciousness, moral status) or
[differences in survey response styles (e.g., acquiescence bias; 34). Although these alternative](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?7t7JTv)
explanations are important to consider, we think they are unlikely to fully explain our findings. Against the
concept-understanding explanation: we defined key concepts in the survey and excluded participants that
failed a comprehension check for the definition of consciousness; comparison entities (e.g., rock, ant)
were generally rated similarly across countries, with substantially larger China–US differences for the AI
entities; and the country differences persisted across a variety of moral status measures, including moral
consideration, rights, and a moral trade-off. Against the response style explanation: we took steps to
minimize such biases in the survey design, such as randomizing the order of scale anchor points; the

8

differences held when comparing relative attributions between countries (e.g., AI vs. ants) and not just
raw ratings; and they held across multiple binary measures designed specifically to mitigate extremeresponse, middle-response, and acquiescence bias. Taken together, we consider it more plausible that
these findings reflect genuine differences in attitudes between the two countries.

We collected several measures that could explain Chinese participants’ greater openness to AI
[consciousness and moral status. Consistent with broader patterns in earlier research (18,21,24), Chinese](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?120pxS)
participants reported more excitement about AI benefits and less concern about AI risks than US
participants, and reported more positive media representation of AI and more experience with AI. They
were also more open to AI friendship and more likely to have experienced an emotional connection with
AI. Aside from some null (e.g., AI risk) or mixed (e.g., media representation) correlations in the US, these
measures were correlated with AI consciousness and moral status attributions. One possibility is that the
greater general positivity around AI in China creates a halo effect whereby AI systems are also more
[likely to be attributed other positive traits (35); in this context, consciousness and moral status. Another](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5iD1VK)
possibility is that Chinese participants’ greater positivity around AI creates less of a motivation to deny
them consciousness and moral status because they perceive less of a need to justify protecting
themselves from AI. This would be comparable to how people may dehumanize human outgroups that
[are perceived as threatening in order to justify their mistreatment and reduce the perceived threat (36).](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ox6BUD)

Differing folk metaphysical worldviews could also underlie the differences. Consistent with earlier work
[(23,24), Chinese participants reported higher general animism (here measured by whether any non-living](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mew1hr)
things can have a spirit or soul) than US participants, and this difference extended to AI-specific animism
(here measured by whether AI can have a spirit or soul). Both measures were positively correlated with AI
consciousness and moral status attributions. Animism could increase consciousness attributions if having
a spirit or soul is seen as a component of consciousness, and it could increase moral status attributions
via consciousness, via another capacity that it may be seen as a component of (e.g., agency), or directly
if it is seen as a criterion for moral status itself. However, general animism alone is unlikely to fully explain
the differences, given that Chinese and US participants rated comparative entities (e.g., rock, ant)
similarly on consciousness and moral status despite differences in general animism. Moreover, the
China–US difference in AI animism was larger than the difference in general animism, suggesting that
there may be something specific to how AI systems are perceived that underlies the cross-country
differences in attributions of animism, as well as consciousness and moral status, towards them.

We ran exploratory mediation models in which excitement about AI benefits and general animism were
included as first-stage mediators predicting consciousness attributions, which in turn predicted moral
status attributions. This model is theoretically plausible and was consistent with our data. However, we
emphasize that this analysis was conducted primarily to make the possible explanations described in the
previous paragraphs more concrete, not as a confirmatory test of these mechanisms. Future research
should test these and other plausible mechanisms, ideally through experimental studies.

Our findings have several implications. Theoretically, they provide evidence that perceptions of AI
consciousness and moral status may not be homogeneous across cultures. Much of the existing research
[on AI consciousness and moral status attributions has been conducted with Western samples (8,14,37–](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nZFd7i)
[40). Our findings suggest that this research provides an incomplete picture, and likely neglects, or at least](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nZFd7i)
under-emphasizes, specific social and cultural contexts that may affect attributions, such as the broader
optimism around AI among laypeople in China than in the US. Building general social and psychological
theories of human perceptions of AI consciousness and moral status will require studying populations
beyond Western contexts.

9

Practically, our findings suggest that there could be widespread consciousness and moral status
attributions among the Chinese public earlier, and to less advanced AI systems, than in the US. It is
unclear how such a scenario would unfold at a broader societal level, particularly as little is currently
publicly known about the views of Chinese experts and decision-makers about these issues. However,
we have seen the Chinese government introduce strict regulations for the design of social AI systems—
the types of systems that would most likely be seen by laypeople as conscious—in order to protect users
[(41), suggesting that there could be strong regulatory action on this issue if it is seen as necessary. In](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?RZz0sr)
[contrast, a more likely scenario in the US may be persistent skepticism about AI consciousness (14), and](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Eh4ErM)
even if such skepticism weakens moral status attribution may not follow, given that around a third of US
participants in our study denied moral status even to conscious AI. This skepticism is already manifesting
in policy, with some US states pre-emptively banning AI personhood and even attempting to legislate AI
[systems as being non-conscious (42).](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FsiBOV)

Although Chinese participants consistently attributed greater consciousness and moral status to AI
systems than US participants, they still devalued AI relative to humans: they favored helping a single
biological human over 1000 embodied, digital humans; they rated an embodied, digital human lower in
consciousness and moral status than a human (and roughly equal to a chimpanzee); and they leaned
against granting conscious AI the right to vote. These findings suggest that, even in China, there may be
aversion among laypeople to granting advanced AI comparable moral status to humans, including to AI
systems that are seen as conscious.

Finally, although our findings largely highlight China–US differences, there was strong agreement for
international cooperation to govern the creation and management of conscious AI systems. Finding ways
to cooperate may be particularly important given that participants in neither country were willing to protect
conscious AI at a geopolitical cost—if governance reduces a country’s international competitiveness, it
may only have public support if it is undertaken multilaterally. However, cooperation on this issue may be
[challenging, given broader strategic competition around AI between China and the US (43), as well as our](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TwNDeh)
findings that people in the two countries differ not only on whether AI systems can be conscious and their
moral status, but also whether conscious AI should be created at all. Nevertheless, just as there is global
[dialogue on cooperative governance around issues of AI safety (44) and AI ethics (45), an important,](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?JbpaZ9)
practical next step is to begin dialogue on the global governance of potentially conscious AI.

This study has several limitations. First, although we recruited samples using quotas for age and gender
(and additionally ethnicity in the US), they likely still diverged from their populations on these and other
dimensions. Generalizations to the Chinese and US populations should therefore be made with caution.
Second, public opinion is dynamic, and could change quickly in the fast-moving and socially disruptive
[(46) context of AI. Longitudinal studies should be conducted in order to track such changes. Third, the](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?AVMrot)
differences reported here are largely based on self-reported attitudes, which may not straightforwardly
[translate into behavior (47). Future research should test for differences on behavioral measures, such as](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?du0bcT)
the treatment of AI systems. Fourth, our analysis of factors that might explain the China–US differences
was based on correlational data. Experimental studies should test these and other possible explanations
causally. Fifth, China and the US are large and diverse countries that contain substantial cultural, social,
[and psychological variation (48). Future studies should therefore zoom in to understand within-country](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?MSQ2d8)
variation in AI consciousness and moral status attributions. Finally, because we compared only two
countries, we cannot tell whether the differences we found reflect broader East–West cultural differences
or features specific to China and the US. Future studies should therefore zoom out to examine
attributions in a wider range of countries. This should include comparisons both across and within regions
(e.g., China vs. UK; China vs. Japan), particularly given evidence that AI attitudes can differ even

10

[between culturally close countries (49). Pursuing these future directions would provide a more complete](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Rn3c2Q)
picture of the expected global response to potentially conscious AI.

_**Participants**_

[We recruited 517 participants from China from Credamo (https://www.credamo.com/) and 502](https://www.credamo.com/)
[participants from the US from Prolific (https://www.prolific.com/) on April 27–29, 2026. Both samples were](https://www.prolific.com/)
recruited using quotas to approximate the demographic composition of the target populations on age and

_**Survey and measures**_

English and Simplified Chinese versions of the survey were created using a forward-back translation
[procedure (50). The survey was initially developed in English, translated to Simplified Chinese by a](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?NoyM6x)
bilingual translator, and then back-translated by a second bilingual translator. Any discrepancies between
the original and back-translated versions were resolved through discussion between the translators and
research team. US participants completed the English version and Chinese participants completed the
Simplified Chinese version.

The survey was administered online via Qualtrics. After consenting to take part, participants answered
questions about AI consciousness, moral status, policy and governance, individual differences,
demographics, and were debriefed. For all measures, summarized below and described in detail in Table
S1, the order of response scale anchors (e.g., "Definitely not" to "Definitely yes" vs. the reverse) was
randomized across participants. [2]

_Consciousness_

[We were interested in attributions of phenomenal consciousness (51) as this is the form of consciousness](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nMUQ1h)
[most typically associated with moral status (26,32). To capture the core idea of this concept in accessible](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CoL6FK)
language, we operationalized consciousness in terms of “real feelings.” We included the word “real” to
ensure participants understood that we meant the AI would genuinely have consciousness rather than
[just imitating it (52). After reading a definition of “real feelings,” participants answered a comprehension](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ollOZ5)
check question testing their understanding of the term. [3]

_Moral status_

Participants read that by “moral consideration” for an entity we mean “concern for their well-being,
protection from harm or suffering, considering their preferences, or treating them with dignity, kindness,
and respect.” This definition was designed to capture a range of theories of wellbeing and normative
[ethics (53,54). Participants then rated whether any current AI systems warrant moral consideration,](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?3uwdyY)

consideration, and made a moral trade-off decision between one biological human and 1000 digital
humans (1–5 scales). Participants also rated AI moral consideration alongside a rock, ant, chimpanzee,
and human (0–100 scale); made binary judgments about whether AI could ever warrant moral
consideration and whether conscious AI would warrant moral consideration; rated capacities that AI must
have to warrant moral consideration (1–7 scale); and rated whether conscious AI should have various
rights (1–5 scale). [4]

_Governance and policy_

Participants judged whether their country should create conscious AI, how they think society would treat
conscious AI, whether there should be international cooperation to govern conscious AI; and whether
they would support protecting conscious AI at a geopolitical cost to their country (1–5 scales).

_Individual differences_

Participants rated their experience with AI; perceptions of AI in the media; concern about AI risks;
excitement about AI benefits; general animism; AI-specific animism; dualism versus holism about the
mind; openness to friendship with AI; experience of an emotional connection with AI; and openness to
social influence about AI consciousness (1–5 scales).

_**Statistical analysis**_

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2). We primarily used independent samples _t_ -tests to
compare mean responses between Chinese and US participants. For comparative measures, we ran _t_ tests comparing within-subject difference scores (e.g., the difference between current AI and ant ratings).
For binary measures, we used Chi-square tests to compare differences in proportions. Where analyses
deviated from these approaches, we report the method used in the relevant section. We also conducted
robustness checks using Mann–Whitney _U_ tests (see Table S5) and using _t_ -tests and chi-square tests
when including participants that failed the consciousness comprehension check (see Tables S6 and S7),
and supplementary analysis testing whether the country differences remain after adjusting for basic
demographic and experience controls (see Table S8 and S9). The overall pattern of results remained the
same across all specifications.
